    The fundamentals behind my map projects: remote sensing, GIS and spatial analysis, explained in plain English, with a little joke here and there.

    Most dashboards answer the question “what happened?”. Boards ask a different question: “what should we do, and why?” If your report cannot answer that second question, it will get a polite “thank you” and then be ignored.

    In this post I want to show how I built a two-page report for Goodnight Inn, a budget hotel brand with 50 hotels, using the Pillow & Key dataset. The brief was simple to say and hard to do: there is $60M to invest between 2027 and 2032. How should we spend it?

    You can see both pages in the dashboard gallery at the top of this site. Here is the thinking behind them.

    Rule 1: every page asks one question, out loud

    The first thing on each page is not a logo or a date filter. It is a question, written in capital letters at the top:

    Page 1: “Where do we stand today?”

    Page 2: “How should we invest to 2032, and why?”

    Right under the question comes the answer as a headline. Page 1 says: “Busy hotels, but we are losing our guests.” Page 2 says: “$49.75M to fix our leaks, then grow.” A director who reads only the headlines still gets the whole story in two sentences. Everything else on the page is evidence.

    This is the single most useful habit I know. If you cannot write the headline, you do not understand your own report yet.

    Rule 2: the subtitle carries the numbers, in a sentence

    Under the headline is one short paragraph with the key facts woven into normal language: our 50 hotels are 79% full, but booking sites now bring 50% of our room money, guest loyalty fell from 8.5 to 3.3, and since 2023 we keep $2.73 less of every $100 a guest pays us.

    Numbers in a sentence are easier to remember than numbers in a table, because the sentence tells you how they connect. “Busy, but losing money and loyalty” is a story. Four separate KPI cards are just four numbers.

    Rule 3: KPI cards need a comparison, or they mean nothing

    Every card on page 1 shows the value and where it came from: rooms filled 79%, “up from 67% in 2021”. Booking-site share 50%, “up from 33% in 2021”. A single number like “79%” leaves the reader asking “is that good?”. The comparison answers it before they ask.

    I also use colour on purpose. Only the bad news is in the accent colour. When a director's eye scans the page, it lands on the problems first. That is the opposite of a “traffic light” dashboard where everything shouts at the same volume.

    Rule 4: name the problems in the reader's language

    I did not call the three analysis tabs “Revenue Analysis”, “Channel Mix” and “Customer Satisfaction”. I called them The Bill, The Booking and The Stay, because that is how a guest experiences a hotel. And I called the problems leaks.

    The word “leak” does a lot of work. A leak is something you can find, fix and measure. It tells the board that money is escaping, and it sets up page 2 naturally: if we found leaks, the next question is how to fix them.

    Each tab uses one chart that fits its question. The Bill uses a waterfall chart showing where every $100 a guest pays goes: booking fees take a bite, running costs take a bigger one, and $33.73 is what we keep. A waterfall is perfect for “where does the money go?”, because it shows each step taking away from the total.

    Rule 5: connect the pages, so the story flows

    Each analysis tab ends with a small note: “Fixed on page 2 by: revenue system, self check-in kiosks.” This tiny box is the bridge between the problem and the solution. When the director reaches page 2, the projects are not a surprise; they are answers to problems they have already seen.

    The footer of page 1 does the same thing: “Three leaks found. Next: how we fix them.” And there is a button, styled like a hotel room key, that takes them to page 2. The navigation itself tells the story.

    Rule 6: show the decision, not just the data

    Page 2 is built around the recommendation: pick 6 of 10 projects for $49.75M, and keep $10.25M in reserve for risks. Four KPI cards show what the board gets for that money: invested $49.75M of $60M, +$7.75M extra profit a year, $34.1M earned back by 2032, and $2.4M a year saved in booking fees.

    A small “Plan confirmed” card on the side lists the key terms, like a receipt: the period, the number of projects, the total, the reserve, and when we break even (after 2032). Being honest that the plan does not pay back fully by 2032 matters. Boards trust reports that show the uncomfortable number.

    Rule 7: let them test the plan

    Directors will always ask “what if?”. So page 2 has two sets of switches: Our plan vs Quick wins only, and Low / Expected / High scenarios. Every card and chart responds. Instead of me defending one number, the board can see how the plan behaves when things go worse or better than expected.

    In Power BI, switches like these are usually built with field parameters, a disconnected scenario table with measures that read the selected value, or bookmarks. The technique matters less than the principle: show the range, not just the best case.

    Rule 8: show time honestly

    Projects do not make money on day one. The timeline on page 2 shows each project moving from Build (spending, no profit), to Ramp (50%, then 85% of its profit), to Full. The colours get stronger as a project matures, so you can see at a glance why the profit arrives mostly from 2031, and why the big room refresh takes longer to pay back.

    This ramp is also built into the numbers, not just the picture. That is the difference between a timeline that decorates and a timeline that explains.

    Rule 9: end with the ask, and show your working

    The last line of page 2 is not “Thank you” or “Questions?”. It is the decision: “We ask the Board to approve $49.75M for 6 projects.”

    Right under it, in small text, is how we counted: an 8% discount rate, profit ramping 50 / 85 / 100%, costs spread over each build, no room money while rooms are closed, all in 2025 dollars. Nobody reads this line during the meeting. Everybody looks for it when they disagree with a number. Having it there turns an argument into a conversation about assumptions.

    What I would tell my past self

    Start with the question and the answer, then design backwards. Put comparisons on every number. Use the reader's words. Link problems to solutions across pages. Show scenarios and time honestly. And always end with the decision you want.

    My take: a dashboard is a tool; a data story is an argument. Both need clean data and good DAX, but only the story gets a decision made. The best compliment I can get from a board is not “nice report”. It is “approved”.

    Some of us have that one report with a beautiful ggplot2 chart or a seaborn heatmap inside Power BI. The one everybody likes and nobody knows how to maintain. This post is about that report.

    Microsoft has announced that R and Python visuals in Power BI, including R-powered custom visuals, retire in May 2027.

    What exactly happens

    In the Power BI service, R and Python visuals stop working in published reports, including reports published before the retirement. So “it was published years ago, it will be fine” is not a plan.

    In Power BI Desktop, future versions will not include R and Python visuals, so you will not be able to add them. Older Desktop versions will still render them locally using the R or Python installed on your computer, which is fine for you but useless for your audience.

    A simple plan for this week

    1. Find them. List every report that uses an R or Python visual. Ask your team too, because these visuals love to hide on page 7.

    2. Ask what each one is really for. Very often it is a chart that Power BI can now draw by itself: a scatter with a trend line, a box plot, a small multiple. Rebuild it with a built-in visual first.

    3. Use a custom visual from AppSource when the built-in ones cannot do it.

    4. Move heavy analysis to a Fabric notebook. Forecasts, clustering or statistical models belong in a notebook, where you can schedule them and save the results to a table. Then Power BI simply shows the results. That is a better design anyway.

    My take: I love Python, but a report should not depend on a script running at the moment someone opens it. Do the data science upstream, store the answer, and let Power BI do what it is good at: showing it fast.

    Scroll up to the “Before → After” slider on this page. That spreadsheet on the left, with #REF!, five date formats, “kumasi” and “Kumasi ” as two different cities, and a tab called “DO NOT USE”, is not a joke. I have received files like that. Probably so have you.

    Every good dashboard starts with an unglamorous job: cleaning. In Power BI, that job happens in Power Query. Nobody posts screenshots of their Power Query steps on LinkedIn, but this is where most report problems are either created or prevented.

    In this post, I will clean a messy sales file step by step and explain why each step matters. No code is needed, but I will show the M formula behind a few steps so it feels less like magic.

    The golden rule: never edit the source, record the steps

    Power Query does not change your Excel file. It records every action you take as a step in the Applied Steps list on the right. Next month, when a new file arrives, you press Refresh and Power Query repeats all the steps for you.

    That is the whole point. If you clean data by hand in Excel, you will clean it by hand again next month, and the month after, until you retire. Power Query lets you clean it once.

    Two habits make this work: give each step a clear name (right-click › Rename, for example “Remove blank rows” instead of “Filtered Rows 3”), and do things in a sensible order, which is what the rest of this post is about.

    Step 1: get the headers and the shape right

    Messy files often have a title row, an empty row, then the real headers. Use Remove Top Rows to drop the junk, then Use First Row as Headers.

    Next, remove what you do not need: empty columns, “Notes” columns full of “fix later!!”, and total rows that someone typed into the middle of the data. Total rows are dangerous, because Power BI will add them to everything else and your sales will magically double.

    Filter them out explicitly, for example remove rows where Store equals “TOTAL”, and remove rows that are completely blank with Remove Rows › Remove Blank Rows.

    Step 2: clean the text before you trust it

    To a computer, “Kumasi”, “kumasi” and “Kumasi ” (with a space at the end) are three different cities. Your chart will show three bars, and your manager will ask why Kumasi is split into pieces.

    Select the text columns and use Transform › Format › Trim (removes spaces at the start and end), Clean (removes hidden characters that come from copy-paste) and Capitalize Each Word. Behind the scenes, one of those steps looks like this:

    = Table.TransformColumns (
    Source,
    { { "Region", each Text.Proper ( Text.Trim ( _ ) ), type text } }
)

    Then use Replace Values for known spelling problems, like “Sunrise 1” vs “Sunrise 01”. If you have many of these, make a small mapping table (old name, correct name) and merge it in, which we will do in Step 6. It is easier to maintain than 40 Replace steps.

    Step 3: set the data types (properly, and late enough)

    Every column needs the right type: text, whole number, decimal, date. Power Query often guesses types automatically with a step called “Changed Type” at the very top. That guess is made from the first rows only, and it can be wrong.

    My habit: delete the automatic “Changed Type” step, clean the text first, and set the types myself after cleaning. Click the small icon on the left of each column header to choose the type.

    Numbers stored as text are a classic trap. “1.2O0” (with a letter O instead of a zero) will refuse to become a number, and the cell turns into an Error. That is actually good news. Power Query is telling you exactly where the bad data is.

    Step 4: deal with errors on purpose

    You have three choices for error cells, and you should choose deliberately:

    Fix them at the source if you can (tell the person who sends the file).

    Replace them with a value: right-click the column › Replace Errors. Be careful: replacing a broken sales value with 0 hides a problem. Sometimes null (empty) is more honest.

    Keep a record of them. My favourite trick: duplicate the query, use Keep Rows › Keep Errors, and load it as a small “Data quality” table. Now your report can show “3 rows had errors this month” instead of silently dropping them. Managers love this, because it builds trust.

    Step 5: fix dates, the final boss

    Dates are where most cleaning projects cry. “12/03/24”, “2024-03-12”, “13/3/2024”, “March 14”, and the famous “1/1/1900” all in one column.

    The first question is: which date format does the file use? “03/04/2026” is the 3rd of April in Ghana and the UK, and the 4th of March in the United States. Power Query uses your computer's regional settings unless you tell it otherwise.

    Tell it. Right-click the column › Change Type › Using Locale…, choose Date and the right locale, for example English (United Kingdom) for day-month-year. The M behind it looks like this:

    = Table.TransformColumnTypes (
    #"Cleaned text",
    { { "Date", type date } },
    "en-GB"
)

    Then handle the impossible ones. A date of 1 January 1900 is almost always an empty Excel cell pretending to be a date. Filter it out, or replace it with null, and add it to your data quality table.

    Step 6: remove duplicates and combine tables

    Duplicate rows inflate your numbers. Before removing them, decide what “duplicate” means. Two rows with the same store, date, customer and amount are probably the same sale entered twice. Select those columns and use Remove Rows › Remove Duplicates.

    Now combine tables with Merge Queries. This is Power Query's version of a VLOOKUP, but faster and more reliable. Merge your sales with the mapping table from Step 2 to fix store names, or with a product list to bring in the category. Choose the matching columns, pick the join kind (Left Outer is the usual one: “keep all my sales, bring in matches”), then expand the columns you need.

    If your data comes as many files with the same layout, like one file per month, put them in one folder and use Get Data › Folder › Combine. Power Query will stack them into one table, and next month you just drop the new file in the folder and refresh. This one feature has saved me more hours than any DAX trick.

    Step 7: unpivot the “wide” tables

    Excel users love wide tables: one row per store, and one column per month (Jan, Feb, Mar…). They look nice, but Power BI hates them, because you cannot easily filter by month or add next month without changing the model.

    Select the store column, then Transform › Unpivot Other Columns. Now you get one row per store per month, with a Month column and a Value column. Long and thin, exactly what a fact table should be (yes, star schema again, I know).

    Step 8: think about performance before it hurts

    Remove columns early. Every column you do not need costs memory and refresh time. Remove them near the start of your steps.

    Filter early. If you only need the last two years, filter the dates early, especially when the source is a database. Power Query can often send that filter back to the database (this is called query folding), so the database does the work instead of your laptop.

    Turn off loading for helper queries. Staging queries and mapping tables that only feed other queries do not need to be in your model. Right-click › uncheck Enable load.

    A small tip from the November 2026 update

    Microsoft is building a new Power Query experience in Power BI Desktop (still in preview). Since November 2026, you can switch between the new and the legacy experience directly from Home › Get Data, without opening settings or restarting Desktop, and your choice is remembered. Try the new one on a copy of your work first, and check Microsoft's list of known limitations.

    My take: Power Query is the least glamorous part of Power BI and the most important. A beautiful dashboard built on dirty data is a beautiful lie. Spend the time here, name your steps, keep a data quality table, and the rest of your work gets easier: simpler DAX, faster reports, and fewer “why is Kumasi split in three?” meetings.

    The short version

    Never edit the source; record steps and refresh. Fix headers and remove junk rows first. Trim, clean and capitalise text. Set data types yourself, after cleaning. Handle errors on purpose and keep a data quality table. Use “Change Type Using Locale” for dates. Remove duplicates, merge instead of VLOOKUP, combine monthly files from a folder, and unpivot wide tables. Remove columns and filter early, and switch off loading for helper queries.

    Every company has this meeting. Finance says revenue was 4.1 million. Sales says 4.3 million. Marketing has a third number and a very nice chart. Everyone is “right”, because everyone built their own measure.

    The November 2026 update introduces semantic views (in preview), and they aim straight at that meeting.

    What is it?

    In a semantic view, you define your tables, relationships and shared business metrics in one place. When you publish the view, Power BI creates a managed, read-only Direct Lake on OneLake semantic model from it. The view stays the source of truth.

    In plain words: you write the official definition of “Revenue” once, and the reports built on it use that definition. Nobody quietly changes the formula inside their own copy, because the published model is read-only.

    Who should care?

    Teams with many reports built on the same data, and anybody preparing for AI. Copilot and data agents give better answers when there is one clear definition of each number instead of five slightly different ones.

    My take: it is a preview, so test it before you bet a finance pack on it. But the idea is exactly right: agree on definitions first, build reports second. It is the star schema conversation, one level up.

    Microsoft is retiring Power BI Q&A in December 2026. From now on, when a manager types a question into Power BI, Copilot will answer. That sounds great until you remember one thing: Copilot can only be as smart as the model you give it.

    If your model has a table called Sheet1 (2), a column called Amt_final and three different measures all named some version of “Sales”, Copilot will do what any new staff member would do on their first day. It will guess. And it will guess with a very confident face.

    This post is a practical, step-by-step checklist for getting a semantic model ready for Copilot and for the new AI agents. It is long, so get some tea. I promise it is cheaper than explaining a wrong number to a director.

    First, what does Copilot actually “see”?

    A lot of people imagine that Copilot looks at your data the way you look at a report. It does not. When someone asks a question, Copilot mostly works with the structure of your model: table names, column names, measure names, descriptions, relationships, and any extra context you have added. From that, it writes a DAX query, runs it, and shows the result.

    So think of your model as a map, and Copilot as a visitor who has never been to your town. If the map says “Road 3” and “Road 3 new” and “Road 3 (use this one)”, the visitor will get lost. If the map says “Kumasi–Accra Highway”, the visitor arrives on time.

    Microsoft gives us a set of tools for this called Prep data for AI. In Power BI Desktop, you find the button on the Home ribbon. In the Power BI service, you select the semantic model and find it on the ribbon there. It has three parts, and we will go through each one. But before we touch those tools, we have to clean the house.

    Step 1: Clean names. Boring, but it is 50% of the work.

    I know. Renaming things is the vegetables of data work. Nobody posts about it on LinkedIn. But it makes the biggest difference.

    Here is my simple rule: name everything the way the business talks. If the sales team says “revenue”, do not call the measure SumOfNetAmtLCY. If the store manager says “branch”, do not call the table DIM_LOC_V2.

    A few habits that help:

    Tables should be plain nouns: Sales, Customers, Products, Branches, Date. Drop the technical prefixes like fact_ and dim_ in the model the users see. Keep those in your data warehouse if you love them.

    Columns should have spaces and full words: Order Date, not ord_dt. Customer Region, not cust_reg.

    Measures should say exactly what they calculate: Total Revenue, Revenue Last Year, Revenue Growth %, Average Order Value. If you have two similar measures, the difference must be in the name. “Revenue” and “Revenue 2” is not a difference. It is a riddle.

    Duplicates must go. If three measures do the same thing, keep one. Copilot does not know which one your finance team trusts, and honestly, sometimes your finance team does not know either.

    Step 2: Hide what users should never see

    Every model has helper stuff: key columns used only for relationships, sort-order columns, staging tables, old measures you were too scared to delete. Humans learn to ignore them. AI does not.

    Hide ID and key columns that only exist for relationships. Hide sort columns like Month Number once they are used to sort Month Name. Hide the raw columns that you already wrapped in a proper measure, so people (and Copilot) use the measure instead of summing the raw column in a strange way.

    This is also where the first Prep data for AI tool comes in: the AI data schema. It lets you choose which tables and columns are visible to Copilot. You might keep a column visible in the report for a power user, but remove it from what the AI considers. Smaller, cleaner schema means fewer wrong turns.

    Step 3: Write descriptions like you are explaining to a new intern

    Every table, column and measure in Power BI can have a description. Most of us leave them empty. Copilot reads them.

    A good description answers three questions: what is it, how is it calculated, and when should I use it? For example:

    Total Revenue: the money from completed sales after discounts, before tax. Excludes cancelled and refunded orders. Use this for all sales performance questions.

    That one sentence stops Copilot from mixing gross and net sales, which is the kind of mistake that makes meetings very long.

    You do not need to write a novel for every column. Start with your most important 10 measures and your main fact table. Then improve slowly. A little every week is better than a perfect plan you never start.

    Step 4: Synonyms, because people do not all speak the same way

    In one company, the same thing can have five names. The sales team says “customers”, the shop says “clients”, the owner says “my people”. A question like “how many clients bought rice last month?” should still work.

    Power BI has had linguistic modelling for a long time, through synonyms. Add the common words people use for your important tables and columns. Think about local words too. If your users in Ghana say “provisions” for packaged groceries, or “chop money” for a daily allowance, and those words show up in questions, add them where they fit.

    Step 5: AI instructions, your note to Copilot

    This is the second Prep data for AI tool, and it is my favourite. AI instructions let you write plain text that gives Copilot business context and rules. Think of it as a sticky note on the model.

    Useful things to put there:

    Definitions: “Our financial year starts on 1 July.” “A ‘new customer’ is someone whose first order was in the selected period.”

    Defaults: “When someone asks about sales without a date, use the last full month.” “Revenue means Total Revenue unless the user says gross.”

    Scope: “This model only covers retail branches, not online orders.” This is very important. If Copilot knows what the model does not contain, it is less likely to make up an answer.

    Two honest warnings from Microsoft's documentation. AI instructions are not meant to change Copilot's personality or make answers different for different people. And they cannot switch off other Copilot features. They are business context, not magic spells. So keep them short, clear and factual.

    Step 6: Verified answers for the questions you hear every week

    Every business has five or ten questions that come up again and again. “What were sales yesterday?” “Which branch is doing best this month?” “How much stock do we have left?”

    The third Prep data for AI tool, verified answers, lets you link a visual you built and approved to trigger phrases. When a user asks one of those questions, Copilot returns your approved visual instead of creating something new. You can also attach filters to it.

    To create one, you select a visual, open its “...” menu, and choose Set verified answer. Then you add the phrases people might type. Add several versions: “sales by branch”, “best branch”, “which shop sold the most”. People never ask the same question the same way twice.

    A small trap: the tabs in Prep data for AI can appear disabled if Q&A is not turned on for the model. Yes, the old tool has to be on to set up its replacement. Technology is funny like that.

    Step 7: Fix the model shape (yes, the star schema again)

    No amount of nice naming will save a model that is shaped like spaghetti. If you have many-to-many relationships everywhere, filters going both directions, and one giant flat table with 90 columns, Copilot will have a hard time, and so will your DAX.

    Aim for a star schema: fact tables in the middle (sales, orders, stock movements) and dimension tables around them (date, product, customer, branch). One clean Date table, marked as a date table. Single-direction relationships unless you have a very good reason. I wrote a full post on this (the one about Auntie Ama's chop bar), so I will not repeat it all here.

    Step 8: Test like a suspicious manager

    Now open the Copilot pane in Power BI Desktop and test. Not with your perfect questions, but with the messy ones real people type.

    Try short questions: “sales?” Try vague ones: “how are we doing?” Try wrong spelling. Try local words. Try questions your model cannot answer, like asking about online orders in a retail-only model, and see if Copilot admits it or invents something.

    For each answer, check the number against a visual you trust. Look at the DAX Copilot wrote if you can. When an answer is wrong, ask why: a bad name, a missing description, a missing instruction, a confusing relationship? Fix the cause, then test again. This loop is the real work. It is also how you learn your model better than anyone.

    Step 9: Mark it “Approved for Copilot”

    When you are happy, go to the Power BI service, open the semantic model's settings, expand the Approved for Copilot section, tick the box and apply. (This setting used to be called “Prepped for AI”, in case you see that name in older guides.)

    This is a signal to users and to your organisation that a human has reviewed this model and prepared it for AI. Do not tick it just to feel good. Tick it when you would be comfortable with your boss asking Copilot anything about the model in front of the whole team.

    Five mistakes I see again and again

    1. Measures with no format. A revenue number shown as 4528193.2271 tells Copilot nothing about money. Set the format: currency, decimals, percentage. Copilot uses it, and users trust a number that looks like a number.

    2. Implicit measures everywhere. If users drag a raw column into a visual and Power BI sums it automatically, that is an implicit measure. It works, until someone sums a column that should be averaged, like a price or a percentage. Create explicit measures for anything important, then hide the raw column.

    3. Dates stored as text. “03/04/2026” is the 3rd of April for me and the 4th of March for somebody in the United States. Store dates as real date types, connect them to the Date table, and the whole “last month”, “this year”, “same period last year” family of questions starts to work.

    4. Codes instead of names. A column with values like BR01, BR02, BR03 is fine for a database. A user will ask about “Adum branch”, not “BR02”. Bring the readable name into the dimension table and make that the column people see.

    5. One model trying to do everything. Sales, HR, stock, payroll, marketing, all in one giant model with 60 tables. It feels efficient. It is not. Copilot has to choose between too many similar things, and so do your users. Smaller models with a clear purpose are easier to describe, easier to secure, and easier for AI to understand.

    Why this matters beyond Copilot

    Here is the nice surprise: everything in this checklist also helps the new AI agents and Fabric data agents. Microsoft's guidance says the same Prep for AI setup is used by both Copilot and data agents. And the agent skills that build reports from plain English work much better on a model with clean names and clear descriptions.

    It also helps humans. A new analyst joining your team will understand a well-described model in a day instead of a month. Your future self, opening the file in six months, will understand it too.

    My take: AI does not remove the need for good data modelling. It makes it more important. In the past, a messy model only confused the analyst who built it. Now it confuses every person who asks Copilot a question. The people who will do well in the Copilot era are not the ones who type the fastest prompts. They are the ones who build models that a stranger, human or AI, can understand.

    The short version (for the people who scrolled straight here)

    Rename everything in business language. Delete duplicate measures. Hide helper columns. Write descriptions for your top measures. Add synonyms. Use Prep data for AI: simplify the AI data schema, write AI instructions, add verified answers. Keep a star schema. Test with messy questions. Then, and only then, mark the model Approved for Copilot.

    Sooner or later, every analyst builds a report about an investment. A new branch, a new system, a fleet of trucks, a hotel refresh. And sooner or later, someone in finance asks: “What's the NPV at 8%?”

    If that question makes your stomach drop, this post is for you. The ideas are simple, the maths is short, and once you understand them you will read every business case differently.

    The big idea: money now is worth more than money later

    Would you rather have GH₵100 today or GH₵100 in one year? Today, obviously. You could save it, invest it, or use it to make more money. And prices may rise in the meantime.

    That simple preference has a name: the time value of money. Because of it, we cannot just add up money from different years. GH₵100 in 2031 is not the same as GH₵100 today.

    Discounting: bringing future money back to today

    To compare money from different years, we convert future amounts into today's value. That is called discounting, and the rate we use is the discount rate. Think of it as the return you could earn elsewhere, or the minimum return the business expects.

    Present value = Future amount / (1 + rate) ^ years

    At an 8% discount rate, $1,000 received in one year is worth 1,000 / 1.08 = $925.93 today. Received in five years, it is worth 1,000 / 1.08⁵ = $680.58 today. The further away the money, the less it is worth now.

    NPV: one number for the whole project

    Net Present Value adds up all the discounted money coming in, and subtracts what you invest:

    NPV = sum of ( cash flow in year t / (1 + rate) ^ t ) − investment

    The rule is beautifully simple: if NPV is positive, the project earns more than the discount rate. If it is negative, you would be better off putting the money somewhere else.

    Let's try an example. A project costs $10M today and earns $2M a year.

    If it earns for 5 years, the present value of those earnings at 8% is about $7.99M. NPV = 7.99 − 10 = −$2.01M. Not worth it, even though you get back exactly $10M in cash.

    If it earns for 10 years, the present value is about $13.42M. NPV = +$3.42M. Now it is a good project.

    Same yearly profit, very different answer. The length of the benefit matters as much as the size.

    Payback: the question managers actually ask

    Payback period is the time until the cumulative cash coming in equals the money invested. In our example: $10M ÷ $2M a year = 5 years. Simple, and very popular, because everyone understands “we get our money back in 5 years”.

    But simple payback ignores the time value of money. The discounted payback uses the discounted amounts instead. At 8%, the cumulative present value passes $10M somewhere between year 6 and year 7, so the discounted payback is about 6.6 years, not 5.

    Payback also ignores everything after the payback point. A project that pays back in 4 years and then stops can look better than one that pays back in 6 years and then earns for 20 more. That is why finance teams look at NPV and payback together.

    IRR: the project's own interest rate

    The Internal Rate of Return is the discount rate that makes NPV exactly zero. If a project's IRR is 14% and the company's discount rate is 8%, it beats the bar comfortably. IRR is popular because it is one percentage that is easy to compare, but it can be misleading for projects with unusual cash flows, so treat it as a companion to NPV, not a replacement.

    Ramp-up: projects don't start at full speed

    Real projects take time to reach full benefit. In my Goodnight Inn report, each project goes through Build (spending money, no profit), then Ramp (50% of its profit, then 85%), then Full (100%). That ramp changes the answer a lot, because the early years, which are worth the most after discounting, are exactly the years with the least profit.

    A useful habit: build the yearly cash flow table first (investment, ramp percentage, profit), then calculate NPV and payback from it. Never type a payback year by hand.

    Real or nominal? Say which one

    If your cash flows include inflation (“nominal” money), use a nominal discount rate. If they are in today's money, for example “all in 2025 dollars” (“real” money), use a real discount rate. Mixing the two is one of the most common mistakes in business cases. Whatever you choose, write it on the report.

    How to do this in Power BI

    DAX has finance functions for exactly this: XNPV and XIRR. They work on a table of cash flows with dates, which fits perfectly with a proper Date table and a fact table of yearly cash flows.

    Project NPV =
XNPV (
    CashFlows,
    CashFlows[Amount],
    CashFlows[Date],
    0.08
)

    Put the discount rate in a what-if parameter instead of typing 0.08, and the board can move a slider to see how sensitive the answer is. For payback, a running total of cash flows by year and a measure that finds the first year where it turns positive works well. Show it on a line chart with a zero line, and the payback point becomes obvious.

    Present it in plain language

    Directors do not need the formula. They need the meaning. Instead of “NPV at 8% is $3.4M”, try: “After allowing 8% a year for the cost of money, this project still earns $3.4M more than it costs.” Instead of “discounted payback 6.6 years”: “In today's money, we earn back the investment in about six and a half years.”

    And always put the assumptions in small print on the page: discount rate, ramp, real or nominal, what is included and excluded.

    My take: you do not need a finance degree to build investment dashboards, but you do need these five ideas: time value of money, discounting, NPV, payback, and stating your assumptions. Learn them once, and the scary question in the meeting becomes your favourite one.

    The short version

    Money later is worth less than money now. Discount future cash flows to today's value. NPV adds them up minus the investment; positive means the project beats the discount rate. Payback tells you when you get your money back; discounted payback is more honest. IRR is the rate where NPV is zero. Model the ramp-up year by year, keep real and nominal separate, use XNPV and XIRR in DAX, and write your assumptions on the page.

    Let me tell you about the first Power BI model I ever built. It had one table. One very wide, very proud table with 87 columns. Customer name, customer phone, customer region, product name, product category, product price, order date, order month, order year, waiter name, waiter shift… everything, on every single row.

    It worked, for about two weeks. Then the file became slow, the measures became strange, and every new question needed a new calculated column. I had built a bowl of spaghetti and called it a model.

    The fix was a simple idea called the star schema. It is the single most useful thing I have learned in Power BI, more useful than any fancy DAX function. So in this post, I want to explain it the way I wish someone had explained it to me: using a chop bar.

    Welcome to Auntie Ama's chop bar

    Imagine Auntie Ama runs a busy chop bar. Every day, people come in and order food. Auntie Ama, being a serious businesswoman, wants to know things like: which dish sells the most? Which waiter brings the most money? Are Fridays better than Mondays? Do customers from the office area spend more than students?

    She keeps an order book. Every line in the book is one thing that happened: on this date, this customer ordered this dish, served by this waiter, this many plates, for this much money.

    She also has some lists on the wall. A menu, with each dish, its category (rice, soup, swallow) and its price. A list of her waiters. A list of regular customers and where they come from. And, of course, a calendar.

    Congratulations. You now understand the star schema. The order book is the fact table. The lists on the wall are the dimension tables. That is honestly most of it. The rest is details.

    Fact tables: the things that happened

    A fact table records events. Sales, orders, payments, deliveries, stock movements, website visits, hospital admissions. Each row is one event at a certain level of detail.

    Fact tables are usually long and thin. Long, because there are many events: thousands or millions of rows. Thin, because each row should mostly hold two kinds of columns:

    Keys that point to the dimensions: DateKey, DishKey, WaiterKey, CustomerKey.

    Numbers you want to add up or count: Quantity, Amount, Discount.

    Notice what is not in the order book. Auntie Ama does not write the dish category and the dish price again on every line. She writes the dish, and if you want the category, you look at the menu on the wall. That is the whole trick.

    Dimension tables: the lists that describe things

    Dimensions describe the who, what, where and when of each event. They are usually short and wide: not many rows, but many descriptive columns.

    The Dishes table might have: Dish, Category, Price, Is Spicy, Is Vegetarian. The Customers table: Customer Name, Area, Customer Type (student, office worker, regular). The Waiters table: Waiter Name, Shift, Start Date.

    Each dimension has one row per thing. One row per dish. One row per waiter. And a unique key for each one. That “one row per thing” rule is what makes relationships clean.

    Why it is called a star

    Draw it. Put the fact table in the middle. Draw the dimension tables around it, each connected to the middle with one line. It looks like a star (or a spider, if you are having a bad day). In Power BI's model view, you can arrange your tables exactly like this, and you should. A model you can read at a glance is a model you can trust.

    How filters flow (this is the important part)

    In Power BI, relationships are not just lines. They are roads that filters travel on.

    When you click “Rice dishes” in a slicer, Power BI filters the Dishes table to only rice dishes. That filter then travels down the relationship to the order book, keeping only the orders for those dishes. Then your measure, like Total Sales, adds up what is left.

    In a star schema, filters flow from the dimension (the “one” side) to the fact (the “many” side). One dish, many orders. One waiter, many orders. Each dimension can filter the fact table, and through the fact table, the whole picture changes together.

    This is why a slicer on Waiter and a slicer on Dish Category can work together beautifully. Each one filters the order book from its own direction, and your visuals show only the orders that pass both filters.

    “But my one big table works fine”

    I hear this a lot. I said it myself. So here are the honest reasons the flat table hurts you later:

    It repeats data. If Jollof is written with its category and price on 50,000 rows, you store that text 50,000 times. Power BI compresses well, but you are still making it work harder than needed.

    It breaks when prices change. Imagine the price of a dish goes up. In a flat table, old rows have the old price and new rows have the new price, and now “Price” means two different things in one column. In a star schema, you decide clearly: the price paid lives in the fact table (as the amount), and the current menu price lives in the dimension.

    Your DAX gets harder. Many DAX patterns, especially time intelligence like “same period last year”, are designed for a proper Date dimension. With a flat table, simple questions need complicated formulas.

    Multiple facts become impossible. One day Auntie Ama wants to compare sales with her purchases from the market. Purchases are a different fact table. If both share the same Dishes and Date dimensions, you can put sales and purchases side by side in one visual, easily. With two flat tables, you are stuck.

    AI gets confused. As I wrote in my Copilot post, Copilot and AI agents understand a clean star schema much better than one huge table full of repeated columns.

    The Date table deserves its own section

    If you take only one thing from this post, take this: always have a proper Date table.

    A Date table has one row for every day in your range, with no gaps, even on days with no sales. It has columns like Date, Year, Month Name, Month Number, Quarter, Day of Week, and maybe Financial Year if your business year does not start in January.

    You can create one in Power Query or with DAX. A simple DAX version looks like this:

    Date =
ADDCOLUMNS (
    CALENDAR ( DATE ( 2024, 1, 1 ), DATE ( 2026, 12, 31 ) ),
    "Year", YEAR ( [Date] ),
    "Month Number", MONTH ( [Date] ),
    "Month", FORMAT ( [Date], "MMM" ),
    "Day of Week", FORMAT ( [Date], "ddd" )
)

    Then do three things. Mark it as a date table (Table tools › Mark as date table). Sort the Month column by Month Number, so April does not come before January. And turn off the automatic date/time option in Power BI Desktop settings, so Power BI stops creating hidden date tables for every date column behind your back.

    Now connect the Date table to the date column in each fact table. From this point on, every time-related question becomes easier.

    Relationship settings, in plain English

    When you create a relationship, Power BI asks you a few things. Here is what they mean without the textbook language.

    Cardinality is “how many on each side”. Almost always, you want one-to-many: one row in the dimension, many rows in the fact. If Power BI suggests many-to-many between a dimension and a fact, stop and check. Usually it means your dimension has duplicate keys, like the same dish written twice with different spacing. Fix the data, do not accept the many-to-many.

    Cross filter direction is which way filters travel. Keep it single (from dimension to fact) as your default. “Both” directions feels powerful and is sometimes needed, but it can create confusing results and slow models. Use it only when you understand exactly why you need it, and you can explain it to a colleague without waving your hands.

    Active or inactive: between two tables, only one relationship can be active at a time. If your order book has both an Order Date and a Delivery Date, you can connect both to the Date table, but one will be inactive (a dotted line). You can switch it on inside a measure with the DAX function USERELATIONSHIP, which is a story for another day.

    A quick “am I doing it right?” checklist

    Can you point to which tables are facts and which are dimensions? Does each dimension have one row per thing and a unique key? Are relationships one-to-many and single direction? Do you have one proper Date table, marked as a date table? Are your fact tables mostly keys and numbers, not repeated descriptive text? Can you arrange the model view so it looks like a star?

    If you said yes to most of these, you are in good shape. If not, do not panic. You can usually fix a flat model in Power Query by splitting out the descriptive columns into their own tables, removing duplicates, and keeping only keys and numbers in the fact.

    Let's fix a flat table together, step by step

    Say Auntie Ama's nephew sends you her sales as one Excel sheet with these columns: Date, Customer, Customer Area, Dish, Category, Price, Waiter, Shift, Plates, Amount. Classic spaghetti. Here is how I turn it into a star in Power Query, without writing any code.

    1. Load the sheet once and call it Raw Sales. Right-click it and turn off “Enable load”, so it does not appear in the final model. It is only a source for the other tables.

    2. Make the Dishes dimension. Right-click Raw Sales and choose Reference. In the new query, keep only Dish, Category and Price. Select the Dish column and remove duplicates. Add an index column and call it DishKey. Name the query Dishes.

    3. Do the same for Customers and Waiters. Reference, keep the descriptive columns, remove duplicates on the main column, add a key. Watch out for spelling: “Kofi”, “kofi” and “Kofi ” (with a space at the end) are three different waiters to Power Query. Use Trim and Capitalize Each Word before you remove duplicates.

    4. Build the fact table. Reference Raw Sales again. Use Merge Queries to bring in DishKey from Dishes (matching on Dish), CustomerKey from Customers, and WaiterKey from Waiters. Then remove all the descriptive text columns. What is left is Date, the three keys, Plates and Amount. Long and thin. Name it Sales.

    5. Add your Date table and connect everything in the model view: each dimension's key to the matching key in Sales, and Date to the Date column.

    The first time, this takes maybe 30 minutes. After a few projects, it takes five. And when the nephew sends next month's sheet in the same format, you just press Refresh and everything flows into the right place.

    What about snowflakes?

    Sometimes a dimension points to another dimension. For example, Dishes points to a separate Categories table, which points to a Departments table. This is called a snowflake, because the star grows little branches.

    It is not wrong, and in databases it is common. But in Power BI, I usually flatten those branches back into one dimension. Put Category and Department as columns directly in the Dishes table. The model becomes simpler, filters have fewer roads to travel, and users have fewer tables to search through. Keep the snowflake in the warehouse if you like; keep the star in Power BI.

    My take: people come to Power BI for the beautiful visuals, and I understand. But the visuals sit on top of the model, and the model decides whether the numbers are right, whether the report is fast, and whether your DAX is simple or painful. Spend the first hour of every project on the model. Draw the star on paper if you need to. Auntie Ama would approve.

    The short version

    Fact tables record what happened and hold keys and numbers. Dimension tables describe things and have one row per thing. Connect them one-to-many, single direction, with filters flowing from dimension to fact. Always use a proper Date table. Avoid one giant flat table, avoid unnecessary many-to-many and both-direction filters, and flatten snowflakes. Your future self, and your CPU fan, will thank you.

    Everybody has a DAX story. Mine goes like this: I wrote a measure, it gave me 1.2 million. I put it in a table next to each product, and every single product also showed 1.2 million. I stared at the screen like it had insulted my family.

    That was the day I learned that DAX is not hard because the functions are hard. SUM is not hard. DIVIDE is not hard. DAX is hard because of one idea that nobody explained to me properly: context.

    This post is the explanation I wish I had. No scary maths, a few short formulas, and a lot of plain English. By the end, you should understand why your numbers change when you move a measure around, what CALCULATE really does, and how to write the time measures every manager asks for.

    Measures vs calculated columns: the first fork in the road

    DAX can be used in two main places, and mixing them up causes half of all beginner pain.

    A calculated column is calculated once, row by row, when the data refreshes, and then stored in the table. It is like adding a new column in Excel and dragging the formula down. Example: Profit per row = Sales[Amount] − Sales[Cost]. Every row gets its own value, and the value does not change when you click a slicer.

    A measure is calculated at the moment you look at it, based on what is filtered on the page. It is not stored. It answers a question like “what is the total, for whatever I am looking at right now?” Example: Total Sales = SUM ( Sales[Amount] ). Put it in a card and you get the grand total. Put it in a table by month and you get one total per month. Same formula, different answers.

    My simple rule: if you want to see it in a visual as a number that responds to filters, write a measure. Use calculated columns only when you need a value per row to slice or group by, like an age band or a “Weekend / Weekday” label. And even then, Power Query is often a better place to create it.

    Filter context: the invisible “for whatever I am looking at”

    Here is the secret that fixed my 1.2 million problem. Every time Power BI calculates a measure, it first asks: which filters are active for this exact cell? That set of filters is called the filter context.

    Imagine a matrix with Product on rows and Month on columns. For the cell at “Jollof, March”, the filter context is: Product = Jollof, Month = March, plus any slicers on the page, plus any page or report filters. Power BI applies all of those filters to the model, and then runs your measure on whatever data is left.

    That is why the same measure gives different numbers in different cells. The formula is the same. The context is different.

    So why did every product show 1.2 million in my case? Because the Product column I put in the table came from a table that was not connected to my sales table. The filter “Product = Jollof” had no road to travel to the sales data. So each row calculated the total for everything. If you ever see the same number repeated on every row, check your relationships before you check your DAX. (This is why I keep telling everyone about the star schema.)

    Row context: the “for each row” feeling

    The second kind of context is row context. It means “I am currently on one specific row of a table”.

    Calculated columns have row context automatically. That is why you can write Sales[Amount] − Sales[Cost] without a SUM: Power BI knows you mean the Amount and Cost on this row.

    Measures do not have row context by default. If you write a measure as Sales[Amount] − Sales[Cost], Power BI complains, because it does not know which row you mean. There are thousands of them. You must tell it how to combine them, with SUM, AVERAGE, and so on.

    But what if you need to calculate something row by row first, and then add it up? For example, Plates × Price on each row, then the total. That is what the “X” functions are for:

    Total Revenue =
SUMX (
    Sales,
    Sales[Plates] * RELATED ( Dishes[Price] )
)

    SUMX walks through the Sales table one row at a time (creating row context), calculates Plates × Price for each row, and then adds up all the results. RELATED fetches the price from the Dishes table using the relationship. Think of SUMX as a careful accountant who checks every receipt one by one, while SUM just adds the numbers in a single column.

    There is also AVERAGEX, MAXX, COUNTX and friends. Same idea: go row by row, calculate, then combine.

    CALCULATE: the most important function in DAX

    If DAX were a football team, CALCULATE would be the captain, the striker and the coach at the same time.

    Its job is simple to say: calculate an expression, but change the filter context first.

    Rice Sales =
CALCULATE (
    [Total Sales],
    Dishes[Category] = "Rice"
)

    This says: take Total Sales, but before you calculate it, set the filter on Category to “Rice”. In a matrix by month, each cell now shows rice sales for that month, because CALCULATE kept the Month filter from the cell and only changed the Category filter.

    That last part is the key. CALCULATE does not throw away all the existing filters. It keeps the ones you did not mention and replaces the ones you did. If the user selected “Soup” in a slicer, Rice Sales still shows rice, because your filter on Category overwrites theirs. This surprises people, so remember it.

    Removing filters: ALL and REMOVEFILTERS

    Sometimes you want to ignore a filter on purpose. The classic example is “percentage of total”.

    Sales % of All Dishes =
DIVIDE (
    [Total Sales],
    CALCULATE ( [Total Sales], REMOVEFILTERS ( Dishes ) )
)

    The top part is sales for the current dish. The bottom part is sales for all dishes, because REMOVEFILTERS clears any filter coming from the Dishes table. Divide one by the other and you get each dish's share of the total.

    Also notice DIVIDE instead of the / sign. DIVIDE handles division by zero quietly and returns blank, instead of showing an ugly error. Small habit, big difference in how professional your report looks.

    You will also see ALL used the same way in older examples. REMOVEFILTERS is newer and its name says exactly what it does, so I prefer it inside CALCULATE.

    Time intelligence: the questions every manager asks

    “How are we doing compared to last year?” “What is our sales so far this year?” Every manager in the world asks these. DAX has functions built just for them, and they work beautifully, if you have a proper Date table that is marked as a date table and connected to your facts. (Yes, star schema again. I will stop saying it when it stops being true.)

    Sales YTD =
TOTALYTD ( [Total Sales], 'Date'[Date] )

Sales Last Year =
CALCULATE ( [Total Sales], SAMEPERIODLASTYEAR ( 'Date'[Date] ) )

Sales Growth % =
DIVIDE ( [Total Sales] - [Sales Last Year], [Sales Last Year] )

    Sales YTD adds up sales from the start of the year up to the date in the current context. If your financial year does not start in January, TOTALYTD accepts a year-end date, for example "6-30" for a year that ends on 30 June.

    Sales Last Year uses CALCULATE to shift the date filter back by one year. If the cell is March 2026, it calculates March 2025.

    Sales Growth % just compares the two. Notice how we built it from other measures. That is a good habit: small, simple measures that you combine, instead of one giant formula nobody can read.

    Variables: make your DAX readable

    Long formulas become hard to read and hard to fix. Variables help. You calculate something once, give it a name, and use the name later.

    Sales Growth % =
VAR ThisYear = [Total Sales]
VAR LastYear = [Sales Last Year]
RETURN
    DIVIDE ( ThisYear - LastYear, LastYear )

    It does the same thing as before, but now it reads almost like a sentence. Variables can also make measures faster, because Power BI calculates each variable only once. And when something goes wrong, you can temporarily RETURN a variable on its own to check its value. It is the DAX version of printing things to see where the problem is.

    Five DAX habits that will save you

    1. Always write explicit measures. Do not rely on Power BI automatically summing columns. Create Total Sales, Total Plates, Customer Count as real measures, and build everything on top of them.

    2. Keep measures in their own table. Create an empty table called something like Measures and put all measures there, organised in display folders. Your colleagues, and Copilot, will find them faster.

    3. Format the code. One function per line, with indentation, like the examples above. Unformatted DAX is like a WhatsApp message with no spaces. Technically readable, practically painful.

    4. Test in a table visual. Before you put a measure in a fancy chart, put it in a simple table next to the columns you are slicing by. Check the totals. Check a few rows by hand. Many mistakes are obvious in a table and invisible in a chart.

    5. When the number is wrong, think “context” first. Ask: which filters are active in this cell? Is there a relationship for this filter to travel on? Is CALCULATE overwriting a filter I wanted to keep? Nine times out of ten, the answer is there.

    A small exercise to make it stick

    Reading about context is like reading about swimming. At some point you have to get wet. Try this with any sales dataset you have, even a practice one.

    First, create Total Sales as a simple SUM. Put it in a card. Then put it in a table by Category. Notice how the same measure gives different numbers. That is filter context at work.

    Second, create Rice Sales with CALCULATE, and put it in the same table next to Total Sales. Notice that every row shows the rice number, even the “Soup” row. That is CALCULATE overwriting the Category filter.

    Third, add a slicer on Month. Click different months and watch both measures change. CALCULATE kept the Month filter because you did not mention it.

    Finally, create Sales % of All Dishes, and check that the percentages in the table add up to 100%. If they do not, look at your relationships.

    Twenty minutes with this exercise will teach you more than two hours of videos. I am not saying the videos are bad. I am saying your own mistakes are better teachers.

    “Can't Copilot just write my DAX now?”

    It can help, and I use it. Copilot can suggest measures, explain a formula, and the new AI agents can even build whole report pages. That is real progress, and it saves time on the boring parts.

    But here is the catch: when Copilot gives you a measure, you are the one who has to know if it is right. If you do not understand filter context, you cannot tell whether Sales Last Year is shifting the right dates, or whether a percentage is dividing by the right total. The AI writes faster than you. It does not know your business better than you. So learn the ideas in this post, and then let the AI do the typing.

    My take: DAX is not about memorising 250 functions. It is about understanding two ideas, filter context and row context, and one function, CALCULATE, really well. Once those click, the rest is just reading the documentation. And when a number looks strange, do not panic. Ask what the context is. The answer is almost always hiding there.

    The short version

    Measures respond to filters; calculated columns are fixed per row. Filter context is “what is filtered for this cell”. Row context is “which row am I on”, and X functions like SUMX create it. CALCULATE changes the filter context, keeping filters you did not mention. REMOVEFILTERS clears filters for “% of total”. Time intelligence needs a proper Date table. Use DIVIDE, use variables, build small measures and combine them.

    “Is there a clinic near me?” sounds like a yes-or-no question. It is not. Near by car or on foot? In the morning traffic or at midnight? Near for a healthy student, or for a pregnant woman carrying a child?

    Accessibility analysis measures how easily people can reach services: hospitals, schools, markets, water points, jobs. It is one of the most useful things GIS can do for planning, because it puts people at the centre of the map. In this post I explain how it works, using my Greater Accra healthcare project as the example.

    Step 1: straight lines lie

    The quickest way to measure access is a buffer: draw a circle of, say, 2 km around each clinic, and count who lives inside. It is fast, and it is often wrong.

    People do not travel as the crow flies. A clinic 1 km away across a lagoon, a motorway or a fenced estate can be a 40-minute walk. Buffers ignore rivers, missing bridges, one-way streets and dead ends. They make access look better than it is, usually for exactly the communities that are already badly connected.

    Step 2: turn roads into a graph

    Network analysis uses the real road network. The computer stores it as a graph: junctions are nodes, and the road pieces between them are edges. Each edge has a length and a cost, usually travel time.

    For my Accra project, I downloaded the drivable road network from OpenStreetMap with the Python library OSMnx. Greater Accra came out at about 105,000 nodes and 275,000 edges. That sounds big, but computers handle graphs like this in seconds.

    Each road type gets a typical speed (a motorway is faster than a residential street), and travel time = length ÷ speed. For walking, every road gets the same walking speed, for example 4.5 km/h.

    Step 3: find the shortest paths, all at once

    The classic algorithm for shortest paths is Dijkstra's algorithm. It starts from one point and spreads outwards through the graph, always extending the closest unvisited node, until it knows the shortest time to everywhere.

    Here is the clever trick for access studies: you do not need to run it from every home. Run it from all facilities at once (this is called multi-source Dijkstra). Every junction then learns the time to its nearest facility in a single pass. For Accra, that meant 306 facilities and one search, instead of millions of separate route calculations.

    Watch out for one-way streets when driving. A trip to the hospital must follow the one-way rules in the right direction, so the search runs on the reversed graph. Small detail, real difference.

    Step 4: connect people to the network

    Facilities and homes are not exactly on road junctions. So each home location is snapped to its nearest junction, and the short walk to reach the road is added. I used the WorldPop population grid, where each 100 m cell holds an estimated number of people, and treated each populated cell as a starting point.

    Step 5: weight by people, not by area

    A map of travel times is nice, but a planner needs to know how many people are affected. An empty hillside 2 hours from a clinic is not a problem; a dense neighbourhood 40 minutes away is.

    So the key results are population-weighted. For Accra:

    About 94% of the 4.8 million residents live within a 15-minute drive of a hospital, in light traffic.

    On foot, only about half can reach any health facility within 15 minutes, and about 820,000 people live more than a 30-minute walk away, mostly on the fast-growing edges of the city.

    Those two numbers tell a very different story from a simple map, and they point straight at where a new clinic would help most.

    The traps (and how to be honest about them)

    Traffic. Typical road speeds describe a quiet road, not Accra at 5 pm. I reduced speeds with a congestion factor, but real traffic data would be better. Always call driving results “best case” unless you used real traffic.

    Data completeness. OpenStreetMap is excellent but not complete. Missing clinics make access look worse; wrongly tagged “hospitals” make it look better. An official facility list is gold if you can get it.

    Edge effects. People near the edge of your study area may use a hospital just outside it. Include a margin around your area, or mention it.

    Distance is not access. The nearest clinic may be full, closed at night or too expensive. Travel time is one part of access, not the whole story. More advanced methods, like the two-step floating catchment area method, also consider how many people compete for each facility's capacity.

    Showing the results

    Three outputs work well together. A travel-time map with clear bands (0–15, 15–30, 30–60, over 60 minutes). A cumulative curve showing the share of people who can reach care within each number of minutes; it shows the difference between driving and walking beautifully. And a short list of underserved hotspots: the places where many people are far away, which is exactly the shortlist a planner needs.

    My take: accessibility analysis is where GIS stops being pretty maps and starts changing decisions. The method is not hard: a road graph, a shortest-path search and a population grid. The skill is in being honest about traffic, data gaps and what “access” really means for the people on the map.

    The short version

    Buffers measure straight lines; people travel on roads. Turn the road network into a graph with travel times, run multi-source Dijkstra from all facilities, snap homes to the network, and weight results by population. Report shares of people within time bands, not just maps. Be honest about traffic, missing data, edge effects and capacity.

    Every GIS beginner has this moment. You load two layers of the same city, and one of them is floating in the ocean near the coast of West Africa, at latitude 0, longitude 0. (That spot has a nickname, “Null Island”, because it is where badly located data goes to live.)

    The problem is almost always the coordinate reference system, or CRS. It is the rule that says what the numbers in your data mean.

    Geographic coordinates: degrees on a round Earth

    Latitude and longitude describe a position on the curved Earth in degrees. The most common system is WGS 84 (code EPSG:4326), the one your phone's GPS uses. Accra is roughly 5.6° North, 0.2° West.

    The catch: degrees are not metres. One degree of longitude is about 111 km at the equator and shrinks to zero at the poles. So you cannot measure distances or areas directly in degrees.

    Projected coordinates: flattening the orange peel

    A projection flattens the round Earth onto a flat map, with coordinates in metres. Flattening always distorts something: shape, area, distance or direction. You choose a projection based on what you need to keep correct.

    Web Mercator (EPSG:3857) is used by almost every web map. It keeps shapes looking right for zooming around, but stretches areas badly towards the poles. That is why Greenland looks as big as Africa, when Africa is in fact about 14 times larger.

    UTM (Universal Transverse Mercator) splits the world into 60 zones, each 6° wide, with accurate metres inside each zone. Accra is in zone 30 North (EPSG:32630), which is the system I used to measure distances and areas in my Accra projects. Ghana also has its own national grid for official mapping.

    The three rules that save hours

    1. Always check the CRS of every layer before analysis. 2. Reproject everything to one CRS that uses metres before measuring distance or area. 3. Never just “change the label” of a CRS to fix a layer. Reproject the data, which recalculates the coordinates.

    My take: coordinate systems feel boring until a hospital appears in the sea. Five minutes checking the CRS at the start of a project saves a whole afternoon of confusion later.

    A land cover map answers a simple question for every pixel: what covers the ground here? Trees, grass, buildings, water, bare soil. Planners use these maps for flooding, heat, farming, conservation and city growth.

    Making one by hand is impossible for a whole city. So we teach a computer to do it with supervised machine learning. In this post I walk through how, using my Greater Accra project, and explain the one mistake that makes many published accuracy numbers too good to be true.

    Step 1: decide your classes

    Start with the question, not the satellite. My classes were: tree cover, shrubland, grassland/cropland, built-up, bare ground, water, and wetland/mangrove. Seven classes that a planner in Accra actually cares about.

    Fewer, clearer classes beat many vague ones. If even a human expert cannot tell “shrubland” from “grassland” in an image, a computer will struggle too.

    Step 2: get training labels

    Supervised learning needs examples where you already know the answer. You can collect them in the field with a GPS, draw them by hand on high-resolution imagery, or borrow them from an existing map.

    I used ESA WorldCover, a free global land cover map at 10 m, as my “teacher”. But existing maps have mistakes, especially on boundaries between classes. So I cleaned the labels: I kept only pixels where the 2020 and 2021 maps agreed, and where all 8 neighbouring pixels had the same class. That removed messy edges and kept 63% of the labelled pixels: fewer, but much more trustworthy, examples.

    Lesson: garbage labels in, garbage map out. Cleaning labels often improves a model more than changing the algorithm.

    Step 3: build the features

    Features are what the model looks at for each pixel. From Sentinel-2 I used the median reflectance of 7 bands across the dry season (blue, green, red, red-edge, near-infrared and two shortwave infrared bands), plus three indices: NDVI (vegetation), NDBI (built-up) and MNDWI (water). I also added the 10th and 90th percentile of NDVI through the season.

    That last pair turned out to be the most important feature of all. Trees stay green through the dry season; grass dries out. The change in greenness separates them better than any single image.

    Step 4: choose a model

    Random Forest is the workhorse of land cover classification. It builds hundreds of decision trees, each asking simple yes/no questions (“is NDVI above 0.5?”, “is shortwave infrared bright?”), and lets them vote. It handles many features, is hard to overfit badly, needs little tuning, and tells you which features mattered most.

    I sampled up to 3,000 pixels per class (so rare classes like bare ground are not drowned out by built-up) and trained 200 trees.

    Step 5: the accuracy trap

    Here is the mistake I promised. The usual way to test a model is to hide a random 30% of your samples, train on the other 70%, and check how many hidden samples it gets right.

    With satellite data, that is cheating without meaning to. Neighbouring pixels are almost identical: same field, same roof, same patch of forest. This is called spatial autocorrelation. If a test pixel sits right next to a training pixel, the model has essentially seen the answer already. Accuracy looks wonderful, often 95% or more, and then the map disappoints in real use.

    The fix is spatial cross-validation: split the area into blocks (I used 3 km × 3 km squares), and put whole blocks into either training or testing. Now the model is tested on places it has truly never seen. My honest result was 84.3% overall accuracy, with a kappa of 0.81. Lower than a random split would claim, but true.

    Step 6: read the confusion matrix, not just the headline

    Overall accuracy hides where the model struggles. A confusion matrix shows, for each true class, what the model predicted. Two measures matter for each class:

    Producer's accuracy: of all the real built-up pixels, how many did the model find? (Recall.)

    User's accuracy: of all the pixels the model called built-up, how many really are? (Precision.) This is what a map user cares about.

    In Accra, water was almost perfect (F1 1.00), built-up was good (0.83), and shrubland was hardest (0.66), confused with trees and grass in both directions. That is useful knowledge: it tells you which parts of the map to trust and which to treat with care.

    Step 7: map, then question your change detection

    With a trained model, you classify every pixel. I made maps for 2021 and 2025, and then made the classic move: compare them to measure urban growth.

    The raw comparison said built-up land shrank by 27 km². Accra did not lose buildings. Digging in, I found the 2025 dry season was greener, so mixed urban pixels (roofs plus gardens) tipped into “grass”. When two maps are each about 85% accurate, comparing them adds both maps' errors together.

    The more honest approach was to count change only where the model was at least 70% confident in both years. That gave a conservative 6.4 km² of new built-up land, mostly on the city's edges. Smaller, but believable.

    My take: anybody can press “train” and get a colourful map. The real skill is in the boring parts: clean labels, sensible features, honest spatial validation, and questioning results that look strange. A model that says 84% and is right is worth far more than one that says 97% and is not.

    The short version

    Choose clear classes. Clean your training labels. Use bands, indices and seasonal features. Random Forest is a strong, simple default. Test with spatial blocks, not random pixels, or your accuracy will be inflated. Read per-class accuracy in the confusion matrix. And be careful comparing two classified maps: their errors add up.

    When people hear “satellite images”, they imagine a very expensive camera taking holiday photos of the Earth. That picture is not wrong, but it misses the most interesting part: satellites see much more than we do.

    Remote sensing simply means collecting information about something without touching it. Your eyes do remote sensing every day. A satellite does the same thing, from about 700 km up, and with better eyes. This post explains how it works, from the very beginning.

    It all starts with light

    The Sun sends energy to Earth as electromagnetic radiation. Visible light (the rainbow from blue to red) is a tiny part of it. Beyond red there is near-infrared and shortwave infrared, and further still thermal infrared (heat) and microwaves.

    When sunlight hits the ground, three things can happen: it is absorbed, transmitted through, or reflected back. A satellite sensor measures how much is reflected, separately for different parts of the spectrum. Those separate measurements are called bands.

    A phone camera has three bands: red, green and blue. Sentinel-2, the European satellite I use most, has 13. Some of them measure light our eyes cannot see at all.

    Spectral signatures: every surface has a fingerprint

    Different materials reflect light differently. If you plot how much each surface reflects in each band, you get its spectral signature, a kind of fingerprint.

    Healthy vegetation absorbs red light (it uses it for photosynthesis) and reflects near-infrared very strongly. To our eyes a leaf is green; to a satellite, a leaf shines in near-infrared.

    Water absorbs almost all near-infrared and shortwave infrared, so it looks very dark in those bands.

    Bare soil and concrete reflect fairly evenly, and they are bright in shortwave infrared.

    This is the secret behind almost everything in remote sensing. Indices like NDVI (vegetation), NDWI (water) and NDBI (built-up areas) are just clever ways of comparing two bands where surfaces behave differently. Machine learning classifiers, like the Random Forest in my land cover project, learn these fingerprints from examples.

    The four resolutions (and why you can't have them all)

    “Resolution” in remote sensing means four different things, and every satellite is a trade-off between them.

    Spatial resolution is the size of one pixel on the ground. Sentinel-2 has 10 m pixels for its main bands, Landsat 8 and 9 have 30 m, and MODIS has 250 m to 1 km. A 10 m pixel can show a football pitch; it cannot show a car.

    Spectral resolution is how many bands there are and how narrow they are. More bands means more fingerprints to tell materials apart.

    Temporal resolution is how often the satellite sees the same place. Sentinel-2 revisits about every 5 days, Landsat 8 and 9 together about every 8 days, and MODIS almost every day.

    Radiometric resolution is how finely the sensor measures brightness, like the difference between a ruler marked only in centimetres and one marked in millimetres.

    Notice the trade-off: MODIS sees everywhere every day, but blurrily. Very high-resolution commercial satellites can see individual cars, but they are expensive and cover small areas. Choosing data is choosing your compromise.

    Passive and active sensors

    Passive sensors measure sunlight reflected from the Earth, or heat given off by it. Sentinel-2 and Landsat are passive. They need daylight for most bands, and they cannot see through clouds.

    Active sensors send their own signal and measure what comes back. Radar (like Sentinel-1) sends microwaves, which pass through clouds and work at night, which is very useful for flood mapping in the rainy season. LiDAR sends laser pulses and measures heights very precisely: tree canopies, buildings, terrain.

    Thermal: measuring heat from space

    Thermal infrared sensors measure the heat that surfaces give off. That is how my urban heat island project measured surface temperature across Accra using Landsat's thermal band. One important detail: this is the temperature of the surface (roofs, roads, leaves), not the air. A metal roof in the sun can be much hotter than the air around it.

    From raw pixels to analysis-ready data

    A raw satellite image is not ready for analysis. The light passed through the atmosphere twice (down and back up), which adds haze. So most projects use surface reflectance products (called “Level-2”), where the atmospheric effect has been corrected for you.

    Then you remove clouds and their shadows using the quality layer that comes with each image, and often combine many dates into one composite, for example by taking the median value of each pixel over a season. That gives one clean, cloud-free picture.

    Where to get free satellite data

    The good news: the best-known missions are free. Sentinel data comes from the European Copernicus programme, and Landsat data from the US Geological Survey and NASA, with an archive going back to 1972. Cloud platforms like Microsoft Planetary Computer and Google Earth Engine host these archives, so you can analyse them with Python without downloading whole images.

    My take: remote sensing looks like magic until you understand one idea: different surfaces reflect different light. Once that clicks, NDVI, land cover classification and heat mapping all become the same question asked in different ways: which bands tell these surfaces apart?

    The short version

    Satellites measure reflected and emitted energy in separate bands, including ones our eyes cannot see. Each surface has a spectral signature; indices and classifiers use those differences. Every sensor trades off spatial, spectral, temporal and radiometric resolution. Passive sensors need sunlight and clear skies; radar and LiDAR bring their own signal. Use surface reflectance, mask clouds, and build composites before you analyse.

    Further reading: NASA Earthdata: Remote sensing basics, Copernicus: Sentinel-2 mission and USGS: Landsat missions

    Pick a fresh mango leaf. It looks green because it reflects some green light. But it does two things our eyes cannot see: it absorbs red light to make food, and it reflects near-infrared light very strongly, because of the structure inside the leaf.

    A dry, brown leaf does neither as well. And that difference is the whole idea behind NDVI, the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index:

    NDVI = (NIR - Red) / (NIR + Red)

    The result is always between −1 and +1. Roughly: dense, healthy vegetation scores around 0.6 to 0.9, grass and crops somewhere in the middle, bare soil and concrete close to 0.1 or 0.2, and water below zero.

    Three mistakes I see often

    1. Comparing different seasons. Grass in the rainy season and grass in the dry season are very different NDVI. Always compare the same months.

    2. Forgetting clouds. Clouds give low NDVI, so they look like “vegetation loss”. Mask them first.

    3. Treating NDVI change as land-use change. In my Rondônia project, about 30% of the area lost greenness, but most of it was pasture drying out, not new forest clearing. NDVI tells you where greenness changed, not why.

    My take: NDVI is the “Hello World” of remote sensing, simple enough to learn in a day and useful for a whole career, as long as you remember what it does not tell you.

    Imagine you want to make a map of Makola Market for a friend who has never been there. You would draw the roads, mark the entrances, shade the sections (cloth, foodstuff, electronics), and maybe colour the busiest areas. Without knowing it, you would be doing GIS.

    A Geographic Information System is software for storing, analysing and showing data that has a location. That's the definition. The interesting part is how it stores the world, and that comes down to two ideas: vector and raster.

    Vector data: points, lines and polygons

    Vector data describes the world with geometry, using coordinates.

    Points are single locations: a hospital, a borehole, a bus stop. In my healthcare project, the 306 health facilities are points.

    Lines connect points: roads, rivers, power lines. The road network I used for travel-time analysis is made of lines.

    Polygons are closed shapes with an area: districts, buildings, farms, lagoons.

    Vector data is precise and compact. It is perfect for things with clear edges.

    Attributes: the table behind the map

    Every vector feature has a row in an attribute table. A hospital point might have columns for name, type, number of beds and opening hours. A district polygon might have population, area and the name of the district chief executive.

    If you know Excel or Power BI, this part will feel familiar: it is just a table, with a geometry column attached. You can filter it, sort it, join it to other tables, and colour the map by any column. Colouring districts by population density is called a choropleth map.

    Raster data: a grid of cells

    Raster data describes the world as a grid of equal-sized cells, or pixels, each holding a value. A satellite image is a raster. So is an elevation model (each cell is a height), a temperature map, or a population grid like WorldPop, where each 100 m cell holds an estimated number of people.

    The most important property of a raster is its resolution, the size of one cell on the ground. A 10 m raster of Accra has a lot of detail; a 1 km raster shows only broad patterns.

    Raster is perfect for things that change gradually across space, without sharp edges: temperature, rainfall, elevation, vegetation.

    Vector or raster? A quick guide

    Use vector for discrete objects with clear boundaries: buildings, roads, administrative areas, facilities. Use raster for continuous surfaces and anything that comes from satellites: imagery, temperature, elevation, land cover maps.

    Real projects use both. My healthcare analysis combined vector roads and facilities with a raster population grid, then produced raster travel-time maps. Converting between the two is common: rasterising turns vectors into a grid, vectorising turns raster areas into polygons.

    Layers: the stack of transparent sheets

    A GIS map is a stack of layers, like transparent sheets laid on top of each other: a basemap at the bottom, then land cover, then roads, then points on top. Each layer is one dataset. You can switch layers on and off, change their order and style them separately.

    The real power of GIS is that layers do not just sit on top of each other. You can analyse them together: which schools are inside a flood zone? How many people live within 2 km of a clinic? That is spatial analysis, and I wrote a separate quick read about its main tools.

    File formats you will meet

    Shapefile is the old classic for vector data. Warning: one “shapefile” is actually several files (.shp, .shx, .dbf, .prj and more). Send only the .shp to a colleague and they get nothing useful. It also limits column names to 10 characters.

    GeoPackage (.gpkg) is the modern replacement: one file, many layers, no silly limits. Use it for new projects.

    GeoJSON is a text format, popular on the web. My healthcare project saves its 306 health facilities as GeoJSON, which is what the Accra web map displays.

    GeoTIFF is the standard for raster data: an image file that also stores where on Earth it is. Cloud-Optimized GeoTIFFs let you read just the part you need over the internet, which is how my satellite projects avoided downloading whole images.

    Scale and generalisation

    A map of all of Ghana cannot show every footpath in Accra. As you zoom out, detail must be simplified: roads become smoother lines, small buildings disappear, towns become points. This is called generalisation, and it is why a dataset made for one scale can look wrong at another.

    The same applies to analysis. A 1 km population grid is fine for a national study and useless for planning a single neighbourhood. Always ask: is my data detailed enough for my question?

    Tools to start with (free)

    QGIS is free, open-source desktop GIS software and a great place to start: load layers, make maps, run analysis with buttons. When you want to automate things, Python with libraries like GeoPandas (vector) and rasterio (raster) does the same work in code. That is how all my GitHub projects are built.

    My take: GIS is not about software; it is about asking “where?”. If you already understand tables and joins from data analytics, you are halfway there. Add geometry and coordinate systems, and your tables suddenly become maps that answer questions a spreadsheet never could.

    The short version

    GIS stores the world as vector (points, lines, polygons with attribute tables) or raster (grids of cells with a resolution). Use vector for clear objects and raster for continuous surfaces. Maps are stacks of layers you can analyse together. Prefer GeoPackage over shapefiles, use GeoJSON on the web and GeoTIFF for rasters. Match the detail of your data to the scale of your question. Start with QGIS, then automate with Python.

    Free satellite data is wonderful, until you have to choose between ten missions with confusing names. Here is the short guide I wish I had.

    Sentinel-2: the sharp, frequent one

    From the European Copernicus programme. 10 m pixels for its main bands, 13 bands in total including red-edge bands that are great for vegetation, and a new look at the same place about every 5 days. Archive from 2015. My choice for land cover, vegetation and anything that needs detail. I used it for my Rondônia and Accra land cover projects.

    Landsat 8 and 9: the long memory, with a thermometer

    From USGS and NASA. 30 m pixels, about every 8 days with both satellites, and two big advantages: a thermal band to measure surface temperature (I used it for Accra's heat island), and an archive going back to 1972 through the earlier Landsat missions. Choose Landsat for heat, or for change over decades.

    MODIS: the daily big picture

    Coarse pixels (250 m to 1 km) but almost daily coverage of the whole planet. Good for national or continental trends, fires and seasonal patterns, not for neighbourhoods.

    Sentinel-1: the one that sees through clouds

    A radar satellite, so it works through clouds and at night. Very useful for floods and for rainy-season monitoring in places like southern Ghana, where optical images are often cloudy. Radar images look strange at first, but they are worth learning.

    My take: start from the question, not the satellite. Need detail? Sentinel-2. Need heat or history? Landsat. Need every day? MODIS. Need to see through clouds? Sentinel-1. And often the best answer is to combine two.

    Further reading: Copernicus: Sentinel-2 mission and USGS: Landsat missions

    You find the perfect satellite image of your study area. Except a fat cloud is sitting exactly on top of it. Welcome to remote sensing in the tropics.

    Optical satellites like Sentinel-2 and Landsat measure sunlight, so they cannot see through clouds. Worse, clouds cast shadows, which look like water or burnt land. Here is how to deal with both.

    1. Use the cloud mask that comes with the image

    Every Sentinel-2 image includes a Scene Classification Layer, and every Landsat image a QA_PIXEL quality band. They flag each pixel as cloud, cloud shadow, water, vegetation and so on. Remove the cloud and shadow pixels before you do anything else.

    2. Combine many dates into one composite

    A cloud covers a place on one day, but rarely on every day. So take all the images from a season, mask the clouds, and keep the median value of each pixel. Leftover clouds and glitches are removed, and you get one clean picture. My Accra heat map used 40 Landsat dates this way.

    3. Pick the right season

    In southern Ghana, December to February (the Harmattan dry season) has the fewest clouds. Plan your analysis around the dry season when you can.

    4. When all else fails, use radar

    Radar satellites like Sentinel-1 send their own microwave signal, which passes straight through clouds. Essential for floods, which of course happen in the rainy season.

    My take: never trust a single image. Mask, composite, and check your data on a map before you analyse it. Clouds are patient; you have to be more patient.

    Making a map shows where things are. Spatial analysis answers questions about where things are. Most of it uses a surprisingly small toolbox.

    Buffer: “what is within X metres?”

    Draw a zone of a fixed distance around a feature. A 500 m buffer around a river, a 2 km buffer around each clinic. Simple and fast, but remember it uses straight-line distance, as the crow flies.

    Overlay: combining layers

    Intersect keeps only where layers overlap (houses inside a flood zone). Clip cuts one layer to the shape of another (roads inside a district). Union merges everything into one layer.

    Spatial join: “which district is each point in?”

    Like a table join in SQL or Power BI, but matching by location instead of by an ID. Attach the district name to every school, then count schools per district.

    Network analysis: real travel, not straight lines

    People do not travel as the crow flies; they follow roads. Network analysis finds shortest or fastest routes on a road network. In my Accra healthcare project, straight-line buffers would have looked fine, but real travel times along roads showed that about 820,000 people live more than a 30-minute walk from any health facility.

    Raster analysis: maths on grids

    Map algebra does calculations cell by cell (NDVI is map algebra). Zonal statistics summarise a raster inside polygons, for example the average temperature per district.

    My take: learn these five tools well and you can answer most planning questions. The hard part is never the button. It is asking the right question and choosing a sensible distance, season or threshold.
